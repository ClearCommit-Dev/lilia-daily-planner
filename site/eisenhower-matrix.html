Prioritising

The Eisenhower Matrix, Explained Simply

With examples · 6 min read

In short

The Eisenhower matrix is a simple way to decide what to do first. You sort every task into one of four boxes by asking two questions: Is it urgent? and Is it important? Then you do the urgent and important tasks now, schedule the important ones for later, keep small urgent tasks short or hand them off, and let go of the rest.

It suits students, busy workers and anyone whose to-do list feels longer than their day. All you need is a list of tasks and five minutes.

What is the Eisenhower matrix?

The Eisenhower matrix (also called the Eisenhower box or the urgent–important matrix) is a two-by-two grid. One side asks whether a task is urgent. The other asks whether it's important. Every task lands in one of four boxes, and each box tells you what to do with it.

Important
1. Do nowDeal with it today.
2. Do laterGive it a time in your plan.
Not important
3. Don't forgetKeep it quick, or ask for help.
4. Unimportant and not urgentPark it or drop it.

The box names above are the friendly labels used in Lilia's Daily Planner. Many guides call the four actions Do, Schedule, Delegate and Delete.

Urgent vs important: what's the difference?

This is the whole trick, so it's worth slowing down.

  • Urgent means it needs attention soon. There's a deadline close by, or someone is waiting on you.
  • Important means it moves your goals forward, such as your studies, health, relationships or work.

They feel similar, but they're not the same. A notification is urgent but rarely important. Starting exam revision three weeks early is important but not urgent. Urgent tasks make noise, while important tasks wait quietly until they turn into emergencies.

A quick test If I didn't do this, what would happen next week? And what would happen in six months?

Urgent tasks cause problems next week. Important tasks cause problems in six months.

The four boxes, one at a time

1

Do now

Urgent and important

Tasks with a real deadline that matter to you, such as an assignment due tomorrow, paying a bill before it's overdue or a sick pet.

What to do: Handle these today. If this box is overflowing every day, that's a sign that box 2 tasks are being left until they become urgent.

2

Do later

Important, not urgent

Tasks that build your future: revising early, exercising, planning your week, calling a friend, learning a skill.

What to do: Give each one a specific time in your day or week. This is the box that makes the biggest difference, because nothing forces you to do these tasks until it's too late. Protect time for them first.

3

Don't forget

Urgent, not important

Small things that are pressing but don't move your goals much, like replying to a group-chat question, booking a haircut or returning a library book.

What to do: Keep them short. Batch them together, set a reminder, or ask someone else to help if you can. The classic advice is to delegate. If you're a student without anyone to delegate to, aim to shrink these tasks instead.

4

Unimportant and not urgent

Neither

Things that feel productive but aren't, like re-colouring your notes for the third time, scrolling "for research" or reorganising apps.

What to do: Park them, guilt-free, or drop them. You can come back to them if they ever start to matter.

How to use it in 5 steps

  1. Brain dump. Write down every task on your mind, big and small.
  2. Ask the two questions. For each task, decide whether it's urgent and whether it's important. Don't overthink it. Go with your first honest answer.
  3. Sort into the four boxes. Most people find their box 1 is smaller than they feared and their box 4 is bigger than they'd like to admit.
  4. Give box 1 and box 2 tasks a time. Sorting alone doesn't get things done. Put "Do now" tasks into today's plan, and spread "Do later" tasks across the coming days.
  5. Look back at the end of the day. What did you finish? What moves to tomorrow? Did anything get sorted into the wrong box?

Common mistakes (and easy fixes)

MistakeFix
Treating everything as urgentAsk: "What actually happens if this waits until tomorrow?"
Sorting but never schedulingGive every box 1 and box 2 task a time slot
Ignoring box 2 until it becomes box 1Book at least one "Do later" task every day
Feeling guilty about box 4Parking a task is a decision, not a failure
Re-sorting all daySort once in the morning and check briefly in the evening

Where does the name come from?

The matrix is named after Dwight D. Eisenhower, the US general and later president. In a 1954 speech, he described having two kinds of problems, the urgent and the important, and observed that they rarely overlap. He credited the idea to an unnamed "former college president". According to Quote Investigator, there's no evidence that Eisenhower used the four-box technique himself. The grid was popularised decades later by Stephen Covey in The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People (1989).

Frequently asked questions

Is the Eisenhower matrix good for students?

Yes. Students juggle deadlines of very different sizes, and the matrix helps separate "due tomorrow" from "important for the exam". The main adjustment is that "delegate" often becomes "keep it quick".

How often should I use it?

Once a day works well for most people. Sort in the morning, or the evening before, and give a few minutes to looking back at night.

What if a task fits two boxes?

Pick the box that matches its next step. A big project can be "Do later" overall while today's small piece of it is "Do now".

Do I need an app?

No. A sheet of paper divided into four works. An app helps you move tasks straight from a box into a time slot and see what you finished.
